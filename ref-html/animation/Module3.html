The 12 Principles
of Animation

The rules that make motion feel alive — codified by Disney animators, universally applicable.

Learning Objectives
  • Name and explain all 12 principles and what each one does
  • Identify which principles are present or absent in a given animation
  • Apply principles to simple objects — not just characters
  • Understand which principles matter most for educational animation
01

Where the Principles Come From

In the 1930s, Disney animators Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston — two of the legendary "Nine Old Men" — distilled decades of in-house craft into a list of 12 principles. They were later published in The Illusion of Life (1981), which remains the bible of character animation.

The principles were designed for character animation, but they apply just as powerfully to diagrams, UI transitions, and explainer video elements. An arrow that enters with anticipation, overshoots, and settles with follow-through communicates more clearly than one that just pops on. The principles are a perceptual language, not just a drawing technique.

Important Framing
You do not need to apply all 12 principles to every animation. You need to know them well enough to choose the right ones. A diagram reveal might only need staging, timing, and slow in/out. A character reacting might need eight principles at once. The art is in the selection.
# Principle Category One-Line Summary
1 Squash & Stretch Form Objects deform to show mass, speed, and impact
2 Anticipation Action Wind up before the main action to charge it
3 Staging Craft Present ideas so the viewer sees exactly what you mean
4 Straight Ahead vs Pose-to-Pose Craft Two different creative processes; different results
5 Follow Through & Overlapping Motion Parts that trail behind or continue after the main action stops
6 Slow In / Slow Out Motion Easing — acceleration and deceleration feel natural
7 Arcs Motion All natural motion follows curved paths
8 Secondary Action Action Supporting motion that reinforces the main idea
9 Timing Craft Number of frames = speed, mood, and weight
10 Exaggeration Action Push beyond realism to clarify intention
11 Solid Drawing Form Objects have three-dimensional volume and weight
12 Appeal Form Designs feel interesting, readable, and engaging
